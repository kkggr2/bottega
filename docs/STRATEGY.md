# Bottega strategy

**One sentence:** your room shows who you are, and your friends' rooms give you a reason to come back.

Everything below follows from research on why people start using a product and why they return. Each rule names its evidence. The product should look simple; this document is where the thinking lives.

---

## 1. Why someone tries it (the first 30 seconds)

| What we know | What Bottega does |
|---|---|
| People judge how a page looks within **50 ms**, and that judgment rarely changes (Lindgaard et al., 2006; NN/g). Visual complexity matters even at 17 ms. | The first screen **is a room**, not a form. One warm scene, little text, one accent color. Sign-in sits beside it, second. |
| Things that are easy to process feel beautiful: familiar shapes, symmetry, contrast (Reber, Schwarz & Winkielman, 2004). | Furniture people recognize at once (a shelf, a turntable, a TV) instead of abstract cards. No new symbols to learn. |
| Beautiful designs are seen as easier to use (aesthetic‑usability effect). | Polish the room first. A beautiful room forgives a rough edge elsewhere; a plain one doesn't. |
| Friends' recommendations are trusted far more than algorithms (Yeomans, Harvard; consumer surveys). | People mostly arrive through **a friend's room link**. That room must open for them without signing in, with one clear "Make your own room". |

**Rule:** a visitor should be playing a record in someone's room before they're asked for anything.

## 2. Why someone fills their room (the first 3 minutes)

| What we know | What Bottega does |
|---|---|
| Talking about yourself activates the brain's reward system; people give up money to do it (Tamir & Mitchell, 2012). | The core act is **"add something that says something about you"**, with a one‑line *why*. The why is what friends come back to read. |
| Rooms reveal personality accurately, especially openness (Gosling et al., 2002, "A Room with a Cue"). Strangers getting to know each other talk about **music** more than anything else (Rentfrow & Gosling, 2006). | The metaphor is literal and backed by research: your room is a self‑portrait. Music is the first thing we invite people to add. |
| Nostalgia raises feelings of connection, then meaning (Sedikides & Wildschut; Routledge et al., 2011). | Prompts that call up memories, not categories: *"a song from a summer you miss"*, *"the film that raised you"*. No blank page. |
| People value what they built themselves, but **only if they finish it** (Norton, Mochon & Ariely, 2012, "IKEA effect"). | A first room must be finishable fast: **5 pieces opens the door**. Import from Letterboxd or Goodreads gets there in one tap. |
| A head start increases follow‑through (endowed progress: 34% vs 19% completion; Nunes & Drèze, 2006). People speed up near a goal (Kivetz et al., 2006). | Show progress as **"3 of 5: your door opens at 5"**, starting at 1 (the room's name and walls count). |
| People remember an experience by its best moment and its ending (peak‑end rule; Kahneman). | **Peak:** the needle drops on your own record. **End:** "Your door is open", with your share picture ready. |

## 3. Why someone comes back (weeks 1–4)

| What we know | What Bottega does |
|---|---|
| Social products die without enough nearby people; build the smallest network that keeps itself alive (Chen, *The Cold Start Problem*). Facebook's "7 friends in 10 days" mattered because of friend density; the exact number is arbitrary (Amplitude; Mixpanel). | Activation = **5 pieces and 3 friends**. Anyone who signs up from a friend's room sends that person a friend request in the same step, and the owner gets a reason to come back and accept. Launch one group at a time (a club, a class, a friend group). |
| Letterboxd users stay for their friends' diaries and the feeling of their own taste being seen, not for algorithmic feeds. | The library is **friends' rooms with what's new in each**, never a feed of strangers. |
| Passive scrolling is tied to envy and lower well‑being; active, connecting use is tied to higher well‑being (Verduyn et al., 2017, 2022). | Every visit invites one small **active gesture**: react to a piece ("this one got me"), save it to your own room, or leave a recommendation by the door. |
| Being noticed creates curiosity and gives something back (LinkedIn's "who viewed your profile"). | Gentle, positive signals only: *"@mia stopped at your record."* No counts, no rankings. |
| Variable rewards drive habits, but the same mechanism powers slot machines (Eyal's Hook model; critiques). | The variable reward is **real**: friends add things when they add things. No artificial loot, no guilt mechanics. |
| Streaks raise short‑term activity through loss aversion, but don't reliably build lasting behavior; the Streak Freeze exists because breaking a streak drives people away (Duolingo). | **No streaks.** Rooms are cozy, like Animal Crossing homes: you drop in when you want. Cozy games meet autonomy, competence and relatedness (self‑determination theory). |
| More notifications correlate with retention but cause opt‑outs (Airship/Localytics vendor data; correlational). | Notify only when **something happened for you** (a friend added a song, someone reacted, a request arrived). At most one summary a week. |
| Yearly identity rituals spread themselves: Spotify Wrapped is a mirror people want to share (self‑concept, social comparison). | **Seasons:** each season the room's light changes and you get a fresh share picture of what you hung. A ritual, not a chore. |
| MySpace profiles (custom styles, a song that played) were loved as statements of who you are. Gen Z leans toward slower, curated self‑expression (Pinterest Predicts 2026, self‑reported). | Customization stays easy and tasteful: themes, wallpaper, arrangement. No open‑ended code, so rooms never break. |

## 4. What Bottega will not do

These go against the research above or against the cozy feeling:

- Public like counts, follower counts or "top rooms" (they invite comparison and envy).
- A feed of strangers, or algorithmic suggestions presented as friends.
- Streaks, guilt notifications or countdowns.
- Autoplay sound when someone enters a room. Sound starts only when the visitor drops the needle.

## 5. How it should look and feel

Don Norman splits emotional design into three levels. Bottega needs all three, in this order of importance:

| Level | Question | Bottega's answer |
|---|---|---|
| **Visceral** (first glance) | Is it beautiful? | A warm, lamp‑lit room with real objects |
| **Behavioral** (using it) | Does it feel good to use? | Dropping the needle, sliding in a DVD, pulling a book off the shelf |
| **Reflective** (meaning) | What does it say about me? | Your room is a self‑portrait that friends step into |

### Visual rules

| What we know | Rule for Bottega |
|---|---|
| Appeal follows an **inverted U** for visual complexity, peaking at moderate levels. Too much hurts far more than too little: the drop is about three times bigger (Cohen's d 2.0 vs 0.6; Reinecke & Gajos, 2014, 2.4 million ratings). Complexity matters more than color. | **Each screen holds one rich thing (the room) and almost nothing else.** Labels, captions and metadata are the first things to cut. |
| The best‑rated pages balance text, color and images; the same complexity score with poor balance rates much lower (same study). | The room carries the image and color. The text around it stays small and sparse. |
| People judge four separate qualities: simplicity, diversity, colorfulness, and **craftsmanship**, meaning how skillfully it all fits together (Moshagen & Thielsch, 2010). Craftsmanship best predicts the overall judgment in later studies. | Details have to be finished: shadows that agree, aligned edges, consistent corners. A half‑finished object costs more than a missing one. |
| People prefer **curved** objects over sharp‑angled ones; sharp contours activate threat responses (Bar & Neta, 2006, 2007). It's weaker for some natural and artificial objects (Carbon, 2010). | Rounded rugs, soft corners on furniture, a round turntable. Sharp edges only where the real object has them (a poster frame). |
| What feels familiar and easy to take in feels beautiful (Reber et al., 2004). **Novices prefer the prototypical**; experts want more complexity. | Furniture must look like the archetype at first glance: a TV you'd recognize instantly, not a clever abstraction. Surprise lives in the *contents*, not the furniture. |
| A layered, restrained return to tactile, physical design (Apple's 2025 redesign; design press). Handmade marks (uneven lines, texture, slight misalignment) read as "a person cared" now that polished visuals are cheap (NN/g, 2025; practitioner view, not measured). | Keep the physical metaphor. Add a few human marks: tape on the poem, a slight tilt on sleeves, handwritten‑feeling notes. **One tactile detail per object**, not textures everywhere. |
| Warm light (around 2700–3000 K) is what people call cozy; several small light sources beat one bright one (lighting practice; little controlled research). | Rooms look lamp‑lit: a warm glow near the turntable and TV, slightly darker corners. Dark mode is "evening", not "inverted". |
| Fonts carry feeling, but evidence is thin and culture‑dependent (Monotype/Neurons, 2023, vendor study). | Keep three voices, each with one job: **serif display** for names and titles (warmth), **sans** for reading, **mono** only for tiny catalog marks, and much less of it than now. |

### Feeling rules

| What we know | Rule for Bottega |
|---|---|
| Teenagers value virtual possessions most for their **metadata**: dates, notes, and friends' comments made photos more meaningful than prints (Odom, Zimmerman & Forlizzi, CHI 2011). | **The "why" is the most important thing in the room.** Show the owner's words on the wall, not the catalog line (type · director · year). |
| Belk predicted digital things would mean less than physical ones; later studies found people do treat digital collections, messages and game items as **special possessions** (Belk, 2013, and follow‑ups). | Treat every piece as an object someone chose: it has a place, an owner's note, and a date it was hung. Never show pieces as a list or a grid of search results. |
| Young people buy vinyl to own something physical and to display it; nearly half say it's worth the price because they'll "cherish it forever" (Vinyl Alliance, 2025; industry survey). | The record ritual is the peak: slide it out, the needle drops, a soft crackle, then the song. That one moment deserves the most polish in the whole app. |
| Looking forward to something adds to the pleasure, especially when the experience is short (Loewenstein, 1987; Kumar, Killingsworth & Gilovich). | Small reveals instead of instant dumps: a 30‑second preview starts after a half‑second needle drop; the TV warms up before the scene. |
| Partiful spread because invites were playful, link previews looked great anywhere they were pasted, and guests needed no account (press and marketing analyses). | **Every shared link and picture is an invitation.** The preview card and share picture show the best piece and the owner's words, and the link opens without signing in. |
| Choice overload mostly fails to replicate; the average effect is close to zero. It shows up when choices are tied to identity and seen by others (Scheibehenne et al., 2010). | Choosing what goes in your room *is* tied to identity and seen by others, so it's the one place to help: offer a prompt ("a song from a summer you miss") rather than a blank picker of six types. |

### Interaction rules

- **One main action per screen.** Anything else waits behind a tap.
- **The room is the interface.** You add a record at the turntable and a book at the shelf, not from a menu.
- **Feedback in 150–300 ms**, with soft easing (microinteraction practice; Saffer's trigger, rules, feedback and loops). Function first, delight second.
- **Touch targets at least 44 × 44 pt.** Overlapping objects (sleeves, books, DVDs) must spread apart or open into a picker before you tap.
- **Words are short and human.** "Your door is open", not "Profile published successfully".

## 6. What we measure (and what counts as working)

Per weekly group of new people. "Activated" = 5 pieces **and** 3 friends.

| Number | Target |
|---|---|
| Room has 5 pieces within 24 h | ≥ 60% |
| Shared the link or picture within 7 days | ≥ 25% |
| 3+ friends within 7 days | ≥ 40% |
| Activated people opening Bottega on days 22–28 | ≥ 25% |
| Visitors to a shared room who make their own | track, then improve |

The numbers live in Supabase: `metrics_cohorts`, `metrics_visitors`, `metrics_daily`. Spend money only once these hold for a group (see the growth plan).

## 7. Screen review (7 November 2026)

I went through every screen against the rules above: the landing page, example room, piece details and share picture in the browser at desktop and phone size; the library, onboarding, your own room, the add‑a‑piece sheet and Customize from the code.

**What already works:** the physical metaphor (instantly recognizable furniture), warm paper‑and‑wood palette, real cover art, the round rug and turntable, the tape and slight tilt on pieces, the serif display type, and the share picture flow.

**Changes, most important first.** Size: S = an hour or two, M = about a day.

| # | Screen | Problem | Change | Why | Size |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Record crate (every room) | Sleeves overlap so much that only the front one can be tapped in its middle; the others show about 21 px. I tapped *Teardrop* and got *Harvest Moon*. | Fan sleeves apart on hover and tap, or open the crate into a row of full‑size sleeves. Same check for books and DVDs. | 44 pt targets; behavioral level | S |
| 2 | Wall placards | Placards show catalog data in tiny mono capitals ("POSTER · WONG KAR‑WAI · 2000"). The person's own words are hidden in the details window. | The placard shows the title and the first line of the owner's *why* in their voice. Metadata moves into the details window. | Odom et al.: metadata and notes make possessions meaningful | M |
| 3 | Add a piece | The "why" is a large optional textarea at the very bottom, under collapsed details, and prompts are generic ("Why this one"). | Make the why one short line (about 140 characters) right after picking, with a memory prompt as the placeholder that rotates ("the summer this was on repeat…"). | Self‑disclosure is rewarding; nostalgia → connection | S |
| 4 | Landing (desktop) | On a laptop the first screen shows posters only. The turntable and TV, the most delightful part, are below the fold. Five layers of text sit above the room. | Shorten the hero to one line and the two buttons; drop the "Try it" caption into a small hint on the turntable itself; lay out the landing room so the turntable is visible on first load. | 50 ms first impression; inverted‑U complexity | S |
| 5 | Everywhere | Mono capitals are used for eyebrows, captions, counts and labels ("EVERYONE GETS ONE ROOM", "PICK A DVD", "5 BOOKS", "@EXAMPLE · EXAMPLE ROOM"). Together they add a lot of visual noise. | Keep mono only for tiny catalog marks. Remove "PICK A DVD" and "PICK A RECORD" (the objects explain themselves) and most eyebrows. | Complexity hurts more than simplicity | S |
| 6 | Needle drop | The record starts instantly with no ritual. | Needle swings in (≈300 ms), a soft crackle, then the preview fades in; on phones, a light haptic tap (already there). | Peak‑end rule; anticipation; vinyl ritual | M |
| 7 | Piece details | The cover fills the window; the owner's words sit underneath in body text; there's nothing for a visitor to do. | Lead with the owner's words, signed "— @name", with a smaller cover. Add the visitor gestures from build step 3 here later. | Reflective level; active use beats passive | S |
| 8 | Share picture | Large empty gap under the room name; posters are small; no human words. | One hero piece large, the owner's *why* as a quote, then the rest of the room smaller. | Every share is an invitation (Partiful) | M |
| 9 | Your empty room | No sense of progress; the first screen is empty walls with "+" buttons. | "1 of 5: your door opens at 5" on the door, starting at 1. First suggested slot is the turntable: "Start with a song." | Endowed progress; music is the first topic between strangers | M |
| 10 | Onboarding | Three decisions (username, room name, walls) before you see your room. | Ask only for the username. Use a good default name ("@name's room") and wall, changeable later in the room. | Shorter path to a finished first room (IKEA effect needs completion) | S |
| 11 | Library | With no friends, the shelf is just you and a username box. The 3‑friends goal is invisible, and you can only invite people who already have an account. | Empty shelf shows two empty spine slots with "Send your link" (opens the share sheet) above the username box. | Atomic network: invites beat search early on | S |
| 12 | Lighting | Rooms are evenly lit. | A soft warm glow behind the turntable and TV, slightly darker corners. Dark mode as "evening". | Warmth and coziness; craftsmanship | M |

**Not changing:** the furniture style, the color palette, the serif, or the share‑sheet flow. These already match the research, and changing them would cost familiarity.

## 8. Build order

1. **Now:** shared rooms open without signing in, with "Make your own room"; anyone who signs up that way sends the room's owner a friend request in the same step; the landing page *is* the example room; tracking for everything above.
2. **Next:** the screen‑review fixes in section 7: first the quick ones (1, 3, 4, 5, 7, 10, 11), then door progress (9), the owner's words on the wall (2), the needle‑drop ritual (6), the new share picture (8) and lighting (12). Finish with the "Your door is open" ending.
3. **Then:** "What's new" on each friend's room in the library; reactions ("this one got me") and "save to my room"; "@x stopped by"; a weekly summary sent only when friends added something.
4. **Later:** seasons with a seasonal share picture; optional paid furniture and room styles, once people return without being pushed.
